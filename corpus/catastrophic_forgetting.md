# Catastrophic forgetting in fine-tuning

## What forgetting looks like measured

Catastrophic forgetting is the drop in performance on earlier tasks or
general abilities after training on new data. Measured properly it needs a
held-out suite from before fine-tuning: for example MMLU, ARC, and a
perplexity slice of pretraining-like text, evaluated before and after. A
typical full fine-tune of a 7B model on a narrow instruction set for 3 epochs
can cost several points on knowledge benchmarks and visibly raise perplexity
on general text, while the target task improves. The drop grows with learning
rate, epochs, and the fraction of weights updated, and it is worst when the
new data distribution is narrow (one format, one domain, one language).

## Why full fine-tuning forgets more

Every weight is free to move in full fine-tuning, and gradient steps on the
new task overwrite features the old tasks used, because nothing in the loss
prices that damage. LoRA forgets measurably less at the same target-task
quality in controlled comparisons: the update is confined to a low-rank
subspace per matrix, the base weights are untouched, and removing the adapter
restores the base model exactly. Studies comparing the two at matched target
performance find LoRA's general-benchmark drop is often half or less of full
fine-tuning's, though LoRA also learns slightly less on tasks far from the
pretraining distribution. Forgetting is not binary; it is a budget spent by
update size and coverage.

## Replay and data mixing

The most reliable mitigation is replay: mix a fraction of general or
previous-task data into the fine-tuning set. Common working ratios are 5 to
20 percent replay data; even 5 percent recovers a large share of the lost
general performance in practice, because the model keeps receiving gradient
signal that its general features should stay. The cost is data curation and
slightly slower target-task learning per epoch. Where the original data is
unavailable, synthetic replay (samples generated by the base model itself,
filtered) is a weaker substitute that still helps.

## Regularization approaches: EWC and friends

Elastic Weight Consolidation adds a penalty lambda / 2 * sum_i F_i *
(theta_i - theta*_i)^2, where F is the diagonal Fisher information estimated
on the old task and theta* is the old solution. Parameters important to the
old task (high Fisher) are held close; unimportant ones move freely. EWC
works in continual-learning benchmarks but is awkward at LLM scale: the
Fisher estimate needs a pass over old-task data, the diagonal approximation
is crude, and lambda interacts with the learning rate schedule. Simpler
relatives see more use in practice: penalizing the KL divergence between the
fine-tuned and base model outputs on a general text sample, or just weight
decay toward the initial weights (L2-SP), both of which anchor the model
without a Fisher matrix.

## Continual pretraining specifics

Continued pretraining on a new domain (code, a new language, biomedical text)
forgets differently: the data is broad, so general abilities erode slowly,
but the erosion compounds over the long runs typical of domain adaptation
(tens of billions of tokens). Reported mitigations that hold up: replay of
5 to 10 percent of the original mixture, a lower peak learning rate with a
fresh warmup rather than continuing the decayed schedule, and periodic
evaluation on the old suite so the trade-off is a chosen point on a curve,
not a surprise. Learning-rate re-warming is a recurring culprit: a sudden
high LR at the start of domain training causes the fastest forgetting in the
whole run.

## Practical checklist

Before fine-tuning: snapshot the base model's scores on the general suite you
care about, and decide the maximum acceptable drop. During: keep the learning
rate as low as the target task allows, prefer LoRA when the task is narrow,
include replay data, and evaluate the general suite at each checkpoint, not
only at the end, because the best checkpoint for the combined objective is
often mid-run. After: if the drop is too large, the cheapest fixes in order
are fewer epochs, lower LR, more replay, smaller adapter footprint; merging
the adapter at a reduced weight (for example 0.7 * adapter) is a legitimate
last knob that trades a little target performance for recovered general
performance.

## Forgetting across RLHF stages

Forgetting compounds across the post-training pipeline: SFT shifts the model
toward the instruction distribution, and RL then shifts it toward the reward
model's preferences, with each stage evaluated mostly on its own objective.
The KL penalty in PPO is, in part, a forgetting control: it prices movement
away from the SFT model. Teams that track a fixed general suite at every
stage boundary routinely find the largest single drop happens at the SFT
stage (the largest distribution shift), not during RL, which surprises teams
that only audit the RL stage. The pipeline view also explains a common
remedy: mixing a slice of SFT data into RL prompts' reference set is not
needed if the KL anchor is the SFT model and the budget is enforced; the
damage was done before RL started.

## Model merging as a mitigation

Weight averaging offers a post-hoc dial: merge the fine-tuned weights with
the base weights (task arithmetic: theta = theta_base + tau * (theta_ft -
theta_base) with tau around 0.5 to 0.8) to trade a little target-task
performance for recovered general performance. Model soups average several
fine-tunes of the same base and often beat each ingredient on both axes.
For LoRA the equivalent is scaling the merged adapter by tau, which is
cheaper to sweep because the base never changes. Merging works because
fine-tunes from a shared base stay in the same loss basin; it fails, often
silently producing a worse model than either parent, when the fine-tunes
diverged too far (high LR, many epochs, different data order at scale).

## Measuring forgetting cheaply

A full benchmark suite per checkpoint is too slow for inner-loop decisions.
The cheap proxy that tracks the real thing: perplexity on a fixed held-out
slice of general text (a few thousand sequences, evaluated in minutes) plus
a few hundred multiple-choice questions sampled from the target benchmarks.
Report both the target metric and the forgetting proxy per checkpoint and
select on the pair. The proxy's job is ranking checkpoints, not certifying
the final model; certify the selected checkpoint on the full suite once.
Teams that skip the proxy typically discover forgetting at the final eval,
when the only remaining fixes are retraining with different settings.
